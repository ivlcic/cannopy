\begin{abstract}
We study when multilingual transfer is practically worthwhile for Slavic named entity recognition (NER). 
Heterogeneous corpora for twelve languages are harmonised to an IOB2 setup with three shared entity types: LOC, ORG, and PER. 
Across three multilingual Transformer encoders, the experiments use manually annotated datasets for eight languages and lower-confidence, automatically constructed auxiliary corpora for four additional languages.
We analyse the effects of multilingual pooling, data quality (manually annotated vs automatically constructed datasets), target resource size, and target language retention. 
An additional same-language Croatian ablation holds the added language and nominal token budget constant while comparing manually annotated HR500K with automatically constructed WikiANN/PAN-X supervision.
We investigate whether a balanced shared multilingual model can replace strong monolingual baselines, whether lower-confidence auxiliary corpora help or hurt, how transfer changes under reduced target-language supervision, and whether full-target or full-pool multilingual training can recover the gap to monolingual models. 
The results show that balanced multilingual training is not a universal substitute for monolingual specialisation, but it can strongly benefit lower-resource targets. 
In the balanced setting, adding lower-confidence auxiliary corpora as equally weighted supervision consistently degrades performance, while preserving full target-language evidence makes multilingual models competitive again.
In this setup, multilingual transfer is most reliable when full target-language evidence is preserved, and heterogeneous auxiliary sources are included selectively.

\section{Introduction}
Named entity recognition (NER) supports multilingual search, entity-centric analytics, event monitoring, linking, and anonymisation.
For Slavic languages, uneven resource sizes and imperfectly aligned annotation schemes complicate both cross-lingual transfer and shared deployment~\parencite{Strakova2016, piskorski-etal-2024-cross, chaplynskyi-romanyshyn-2024-introducing}.
Multilingual encoders make zero- and few-shot transfer feasible~\parencite{rahimi-etal-2019-massively, pollak-etal-2021-embeddia, pelicon-etal-2021-zero}, while a shared model can simplify deployment by replacing several monolingual systems~\parencite{suppa2021benchmarking, prelevikj-zitnik-2021-multilingual}.
However, effectiveness varies by task and language~\parencite{ulcar2026monocross}, leaving open whether multilingual fine-tuning remains useful once labelled target-language data are available.

Prior work examined whether incorporating closely related Slavic languages during fine-tuning improves target-language NER ~\parencite{ivacic-etal-2023-analysis}. 
The results were mixed rather than uniformly favourable: multilingual training did not improve Slovene, yielded gains for Croatian only in selected configurations, and produced the clearest improvement for Serbian, which had the smallest target corpus. 
These findings suggest that the benefits of multilingual transfer are likely to be strongest in lower-resource settings, while underscoring the practical appeal of shared multilingual models for simplicity and scalability.

These observations motivate a more targeted follow-up question: not simply whether adding languages is beneficial, but under which conditions additional languages improve performance, when they instead introduce negative transfer, and how these effects depend on corpus quality, label-space compatibility, and the amount of target-language supervision.

The question also matters operationally.
High-volume, repeated, or latency-sensitive extraction workloads favour compact sequence-labelling models that are efficient to batch, retrain, and redeploy when data or annotation schemes change~\parencite{ivacic-etal-2025-extreme}.

This work makes two contributions. 
First, it extends the earlier South-Slavic transfer analysis into a broader multilingual study centred on balanced pooled training, heterogeneous corpora, target-resource settings, target-retention experiments, and full-pool shared models. 
Second, it turns these comparisons into practical guidance on when to use monolingual specialisation, balanced multilingual transfer, target-specific multilingual training, or a single shared full-pool model. 

Five research questions guide this study. 
The first three evaluate balanced multilingual transfer directly, while the final two separate multilingual transfer from the loss of target-language evidence caused by balancing.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{RQ\arabic*.}, leftmargin=*, itemsep=-2pt, topsep=-3pt]
    \item Can a single balanced shared multilingual model match or outperform strong full-data monolingual baselines on the eight main evaluation languages?
    We compare separate full-data monolingual models, one per target (\texttt{Mono-L}), with one balanced shared model over the eight main languages (\texttt{Multi-8}) on Bulgarian, Czech, Croatian, Polish, Russian, Slovene, Serbian, and Ukrainian.
    \item Does adding lower-confidence automatically constructed data to balanced multilingual training help or cause negative transfer, and does source quality matter?
    We compare the balanced eight-language model with its twelve-language counterpart (\texttt{Multi-12}), then isolate source quality through a Croatian ablation with the same nominal token budget.
    \item How does the performance gap between monolingual and balanced multilingual training change across relative target-resource budgets?
    We construct relative-budget profiles for Serbian and Slovene at 10\%, 25\%, 50\%, and 100\%, where monolingual budgets are fractions of the full target-language training set and multilingual budgets are fractions of the balanced target-language quota.
    \item When a balanced shared multilingual model underperforms monolingual baselines, is the loss mainly caused by target-language downsampling, and can cross-lingual transfer still help through target-specific training?
    We compare the monolingual and balanced shared baselines with target-specific multilingual models that retain all target data (\texttt{Multi8-full-L}) and with leave-one-target-out multilingual pretraining followed by full-target adaptation (\texttt{Pretrain-Multi7-full-L}).
    \item For practical deployment, can a full-pool shared multilingual model recover monolingual-level performance, and does capping lower-confidence auxiliary data improve over adding them in full?
    We compare shared models trained on the complete eight- and twelve-language pools (\texttt{Full-Multi8} and \texttt{Full-Multi12}) with a twelve-language variant that caps the auxiliary contribution (\texttt{Full-Multi12-CapAux}).
\end{enumerate}

The working hypothesis behind these questions is that multilingual transfer is most useful for less-represented target languages, but only when the added data are sufficiently compatible and when training does not remove too much target-language evidence.
In other words, the central trade-off is not simply monolingual versus multilingual training, but quality, target evidence, and transfer recipe.

\section{Related work}
Research on NER for Slavic languages covers monolingual systems, annotated corpora, and shared tasks. 
Work on Croatian and Slovene included CroNER~\parencite{karan-2013-croner}, joint Croatian--Slovene CRF-based NER modelling~\parencite{ljubevsic2013combining}, NER for Croatian tweets~\parencite{baksa2016tagging}, and the manually annotated SETimes.HR corpus~\parencite{agic2014setimes}. 
Additional resources have been developed for Slovene~\parencite{krek2020a-ssj500k}, Croatian~\parencite{hr500k-10}, and Serbian~\parencite{SETimes-SR-1.0}.

Czech NER resources include CNEC 2.0, which contains 8{,}993 sentences annotated with 35{,}220 named entities, as well as CRF-based recognition work~\parencite{CNEC-2.0, konkol2013crf}.
In Polish, prior work has introduced annotation schemes and resources for the National Corpus of Polish~\parencite{waszczuk2010tools, savary2011language}, as well as methods for named-entity lemmatisation~\parencite{piskorski2009knowledge, marcinczuk2017lemmatization}. 

Polish NER has also been evaluated in shared-task settings: PolEval 2018~\parencite{ogrodniczuk-poleval-2018} included an NER task, while PolEval 2020~\parencite{ogrodniczuk-poleval-2020} focused more broadly on information extraction and entity typing.

For Russian, key benchmarks include FactRuEval 2016~\parencite{as2016factrueval} and RuNNE-2022~\parencite{artemova2022runne}, the latter of which is built on the NEREL dataset~\parencite{loukachevitch-etal-2021-nerel}. 
At the multilingual level, SemEval 2022 Task 11 (MultiCoNER) includes Russian~\parencite{malmasi2022semeval}, and SemEval 2023 Task 2 (MultiCoNER 2) includes Ukrainian~\parencite{fetahu2023semeval}.

More broadly, the BSNLP/Slav-NER shared tasks from 2017 to 2023 advance cross-lingual NER, normalisation, and linking for Slavic languages. 
The 2024 cross-lingual Slavic named entity corpus~\parencite{piskorski-etal-2024-cross} was created by merging and curating data from the \textcite{piskorski-etal-2017-first, piskorski-etal-2019-second, piskorski-etal-2021-slav, yangarber-etal-2023-slav} editions of those shared tasks, adding positional information for entity mentions.

\subsection{Data preparation}
\label{sec:corpus_prep}
To enable multilingual pooling, we first converted all corpora to a common sentence-level IOB2 representation.
For languages represented by multiple source corpora, we pool all prepared sentences that share a language data key before constructing the split; we do not split each source corpus separately and then pool the resulting partitions.
For each random seed, the pooled sentences within each data key were randomly divided into training, validation, and test partitions using an 80/10/10 ratio.
Exact sentence duplicates were then removed independently within each data key.
Duplicate matching uses the NFKC-normalised, case-folded token sequence; when a duplicate crosses partitions, the test occurrence is retained over validation and training, and validation is retained over training.
Repetitions within a partition are also removed.
The manually annotated Croatian and Croatian WikiANN sources instead retain separate data keys, so they are split independently and are not deduplicated against one another.
Across all processed corpora, including the Croatian WikiANN corpus used only in the source ablation, deduplication removed 48,225 of 331,498 sentence instances (14.55\%), leaving 283,273.

The deduplicated samples used in the NER experiments are subsequently harmonised into a common coarse-grained label space.
We use three entity types, \texttt{PER}, \texttt{ORG}, and \texttt{LOC}, as these are the only labels consistently available across all 12 languages. 
Corpus-specific fine-grained labels were mapped to the nearest coarse category when the mapping was clear.
Remaining unsupported entity labels, including \texttt{MISC}, were mapped to \texttt{O}, while their tokens and sentences were retained.
This choice reduces label-space asymmetry in multilingual training and makes the comparison between monolingual and multilingual models more interpretable.

After harmonisation, the core twelve-language training collection remains strongly imbalanced in both size and annotation density (see Figure~\ref{fig:entity-density-by-language}).
The figure and the following descriptive statistics use the \texttt{Full-Multi12} training split for a single seed after splitting, deduplication, and label harmonisation.
They exclude the additional Croatian WikiANN/PAN-X source, which was used only in the ablation.
When we collapse \texttt{B-} and \texttt{I-} prefixes and consider only \texttt{LOC}, \texttt{ORG}, and \texttt{PER}, the four auxiliary datasets for Bosnian, Macedonian, Slovak, and Albanian contribute 494{,}749 tokens, i.e.\ only 15.1\% of all training tokens in this collection, but 158{,}514 entity-labelled tokens, i.e.\ 40.7\% of all \texttt{LOC}/\texttt{ORG}/\texttt{PER} labels.
By contrast, the eight main language-specific datasets contribute 84.9\% of all tokens and 59.3\% of the \texttt{LOC}/\texttt{ORG}/\texttt{PER}-labelled tokens.
This difference is reflected in annotation density: the auxiliary group contains 320.4 entity-labelled tokens per 1{,}000 tokens, compared with only 83.1 per 1{,}000 tokens in the main group.
The auxiliary corpora also have much shorter average sentence length (9.2 vs 20.3 tokens per sentence), which indicates that the multilingual training signal differs not only in corpus provenance but also in segmentation and annotation style.

At the level of label composition, the main and auxiliary groups remain broadly similar once \texttt{MISC} is removed.
In the main group, \texttt{LOC}, \texttt{ORG}, and \texttt{PER} account for 28.6\%, 37.5\%, and 33.9\% of all collapsed entity label tokens, respectively; in the auxiliary group, the corresponding shares are 32.8\%, 33.3\%, and 33.9\%.
Label composition suggests that the main source of mismatch is not the relative balance between the three shared entity types, but rather the much higher annotation density and shorter sentence segmentation in the auxiliary corpora (see Figure~\ref{fig:label-composition-by-language}). 
For that reason, naive concatenation can overexpose a multilingual model to auxiliary corpora entity supervision. 
We therefore treat the sampling strategy as an explicit experimental factor. 
The balanced multilingual settings equalise language exposure and make cross-language comparisons diagnostic. At the same time, the latter full-target and full-pool variants test whether preserving the full target-language evidence changes the practical conclusion.


\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Task setup}
We cast named entity recognition (NER) as token-level sequence labelling on tokenised sentences, using the IOB2 scheme.
The classifier predicts seven tags: the outside label \texttt{O}, together with \texttt{B-} and \texttt{I-} tags for each of the three coarse-grained entity types \texttt{LOC}, \texttt{ORG}, and \texttt{PER}.

We consider 12 languages: Bulgarian, Bosnian, Czech, Croatian, Macedonian, Polish, Russian, Slovak, Slovene, Albanian, Serbian, and Ukrainian. We denote the corresponding set of language codes as
\[
L_{12} = \{\texttt{bg}, \texttt{bs}, \texttt{cs}, \texttt{hr}, \texttt{mk}, \texttt{pl}, \texttt{ru}, \texttt{sk}, \texttt{sl}, \texttt{sq}, \texttt{sr}, \texttt{uk}\}.
\]
Following the data-quality distinction introduced in Section~\ref{sec:data}, we define eight main evaluation language datasets and four lower-confidence auxiliary datasets in additional languages:
\[
L_8 = \{\texttt{bg}, \texttt{cs}, \texttt{hr}, \texttt{pl}, \texttt{ru}, \texttt{sl}, \texttt{sr}, \texttt{uk}\}
\text{ and  }
A = \{\texttt{bs}, \texttt{mk}, \texttt{sk}, \texttt{sq}\}.
\]
All evaluation languages belong to $L_8$, while the auxiliary group $A$ is used only within the multilingual training pool to test whether adding noisier cross-lingual supervision improves or degrades a shared model.
The shared core comparisons are reported over all languages in $L_8$, whereas the target-resource profiles are restricted to Serbian and Slovene.

For the controlled Croatian source-quality ablation, we define
\[
L_7 = L_8 \setminus \{\texttt{hr}\}.
\]
Starting from the same balanced $L_7$ pool (\texttt{Multi7-no-hr}), we add either manually annotated \texttt{HR500K} (\texttt{Multi7+HR500K}) or Croatian \texttt{WikiANN/PAN-X} (\texttt{Multi7+HR-WikiANN}).
All three conditions are evaluated on $L_7$.
Within each seed, all three conditions use the same nominal per-language token budget, determined by the smallest $L_7$ training split.
Neither Croatian source is oversampled.

A direct concatenation of all corpora would cause large datasets to dominate multilingual training.
After each seed-specific split and deduplication, we therefore balance every multilingual pool to the training-token count of its smallest language.
%Serbian determines the per-language budget for \texttt{Multi-8}, which ranges from 69{,}115 to 69{,}743 tokens across the three seeds; Albanian determines it for \texttt{Multi-12}, where it ranges from 34{,}664 to 35{,}428 tokens.
Serbian sets the per-language budget for \texttt{Multi-8}, with values ranging from 69{,}115 to 69{,}743 tokens across the three seeds, while Albanian sets the corresponding budget for \texttt{Multi-12}, where it ranges from 34{,}664 to 35{,}428 tokens.
For each language, we shuffle the training sentences and sample without replacement until this budget is reached or first exceeded.
Retaining complete sentences can cause a small overshoot.
Within a run, the resulting training pool remains fixed across epochs, while batches are reshuffled.

This procedure keeps exposure comparable across languages, but it can also heavily downsample large target languages. 
We therefore add full-target and full-pool variants that deliberately relax this balancing constraint to distinguish negative transfer from the loss of target-language evidence.

\subsection{Experimental settings}
Across three pretrained multilingual Transformer encoder architectures, we evaluate seven families of training configurations.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=-2pt, topsep=-3pt]
%\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0.6em}
    \item \textbf{Monolingual baselines (\texttt{Mono-L}).}
    For each evaluation language $L \in L_8$, we train one monolingual model using only the full training set of that language.
    These runs provide strong practical baselines and reflect the best single-language setting available for each target.
    Because corpus sizes differ substantially across languages, these baselines are not token-matched across $L$; instead, each language uses all available target-language data.

    \item \textbf{Balanced shared multilingual model on eight languages (\texttt{Multi-8}).}
    We train one shared multilingual model on the eight higher-confidence evaluation languages in $L_8$ using balanced per-language sampling.
    This condition tests whether a compact shared multilingual model trained on the cleaner subset can match or outperform the corresponding monolingual baselines.

    \item \textbf{Balanced shared multilingual model on twelve languages (\texttt{Multi-12}).}
    We train one shared multilingual model on the full language pool $L_{12} = L_8 \cup A$, again using balanced per-language sampling. 
    This condition tests the balanced twelve-language design: it adds the four lower-confidence auxiliary corpora and, because the quota is recomputed over $L_{12}$, uses a lower per-language quota for the main languages than \texttt{Multi-8}.

    \item \textbf{Controlled Croatian source-quality ablation.}
    We compare \texttt{Multi7-no-hr}, \texttt{Multi7+HR500K}, and \texttt{Multi7+HR-WikiANN} to isolate source quality while holding the added language and nominal token budget constant.

    \item \textbf{Target-specific full-target multilingual models (\texttt{Multi8-full-L}).}
    For each target language $L \in L_8$, we train a target-specific multilingual model that retains all available training data for $L$ while giving each of the other seven higher-confidence datasets the same seed-specific token budget used in \texttt{Multi-8}.
    This condition asks whether clean multilingual augmentation helps once the target language is no longer downsampled.

    \item \textbf{Leave-one-target-out multilingual pretraining (\texttt{Pretrain-Multi7-full-L}).}
    For each target language $L \in L_8$, we first train a balanced multilingual model on the seven higher-confidence datasets in $L_8 \setminus \{L\}$, excluding the target language entirely and recomputing the common quota over the remaining languages.
    We then continue fine-tuning on the full target-language training set for $L$.
    This two-stage setup tests whether the other clean Slavic languages provide a useful pretraining signal before target-only adaptation.

    \item \textbf{Full-pool shared multilingual models.}
    Finally, we train shared multilingual models that preserve all available main-language data rather than balancing each language down to a common quota. \texttt{Full-Multi8} uses the complete $L_8$ pool.
    \texttt{Full-Multi12} adds all four auxiliary languages.
    \texttt{Full-Multi12-CapAux} keeps the full $L_8$ pool but caps each auxiliary dataset at the seed-specific per-language budget used in \texttt{Multi-12}, testing whether lower-confidence WikiANN/PAN-X-derived data are harmful because they are added at all or because they receive too much effective weight.
\end{enumerate}

Together, these settings separate three effects that are otherwise confounded: multilingual transfer, source-data quality, and the amount of target-language evidence retained during training.

\subsection{Target-resource profiles}
To analyse how the performance gap between monolingual and balanced multilingual training changes with target-resource availability, we construct relative-budget profiles for Serbian (\texttt{sr}) and Slovene (\texttt{sl}).
These languages were chosen because they represent opposite ends of the clean-data spectrum in our setup: Serbian is the smallest higher-confidence target language dataset, while Slovene is the largest.

For each target language $g \in \{\texttt{sr}, \texttt{sl}\}$ and seed $s$, we use one seed-specific shuffle of the target-language training sentences to construct nested subsets
\[
D_{g,s}^{10} \subset D_{g,s}^{25} \subset D_{g,s}^{50} \subset D_{g,s}^{100} = D_{g,s}^{\mathrm{train}}.
\]
For the monolingual profile, at each percentage $p \in \{10,25,50,100\}$, we retain complete sentences from the same seed-specific shuffled order until $p$ per cent of the tokens in the full target-language training split is first reached or exceeded.
Reusing the same sentence order makes the resulting subsets nested, while complete-sentence sampling can place the realised token count slightly above the nominal budget.
The \texttt{Mono-g@p} model is trained only on the resulting subset.

For the multilingual profiles, we reduce only the target-language contribution while keeping the non-target-language samples unchanged.
At each nominal percentage, \texttt{Multi-8-g@p} and \texttt{Multi-12-g@p} retain that percentage of the target-language sample used by the corresponding balanced model, while every non-target language retains its complete balanced sample.
Complete-sentence sampling can again cause a small overshoot. The $p=100$ points reuse the standard \texttt{Mono-g}, \texttt{Multi-8}, and \texttt{Multi-12} models; only the $10\%$, $25\%$, and $50\%$ points require additional profile runs.

The same nominal percentage therefore represents a relative-budget comparison and is not generally target-token matched between monolingual and multilingual profiles.
Serbian \texttt{Mono} versus \texttt{Multi-8} is the exception because Serbian is the smallest language in $L_8$ and therefore determines the balanced per-language budget.
The discrepancy is especially large for Slovene.
Within each configuration family, reducing $p$ decreases both the amount of unique target-language evidence and its effective weight in the multilingual pool, providing a controlled profile of target-data scarcity.

\subsection{Model and training configuration}
We evaluate three pretrained multilingual Transformer encoders: \texttt{mmBERT} (\texttt{jhu-clsp/mmBERT-base})~\parencite{marone2025mmbertmodernmultilingualencoder}, \texttt{mDeBERTa-v3} (\texttt{microsoft/mdeberta-v3-base})~\parencite{he2021debertav3}, and \texttt{XLM-R} (\texttt{FacebookAI/xlm-roberta-base})~\parencite{conneau2019xlmr}.
The checkpoints are loaded through the Hugging Face Transformers library~\parencite{wolf-etal-2020-transformers} with their corresponding token-classification architectures.

Before running the full experiment grid, we select the learning rate and dropout through a bounded \texttt{Multi-8} validation sweep that also includes \texttt{BERTi\'{c}} (\texttt{classla/bcms-bertic})~\parencite{ljubesic-lauc-2021-bertic} as a fourth candidate encoder.
For \texttt{mmBERT}, we test learning rates of $\{1,2,3\} \times 10^{-5}$ with dropout values of 0.05 and 0.10;
for \texttt{mDeBERTa-v3}, we test $\{1,1.5,2,2.5\} \times 10^{-5}$ with dropout values of 0.10 and 0.15;
for \texttt{XLM-R}, we test $\{1,2\} \times 10^{-5}$ with dropout values of 0.10 and 0.15;
and for \texttt{BERTi\'{c}}, we test $\{1,3,5,10\} \times 10^{-5}$ with dropout fixed at 0.10.
The sweep therefore contains 22 candidate settings: six for \texttt{mmBERT}, eight for \texttt{mDeBERTa-v3}, four for \texttt{XLM-R}, and four for \texttt{BERTi\'{c}}.
Each candidate setting is trained for up to 15 epochs with three random seeds and ranked by mean macro validation F1 over $L_8$; test scores are not used for selection.
The best \texttt{BERTi\'{c}} setting reaches 89.71 validation macro F1, compared with 91.41 for \texttt{mmBERT}, 91.90 for \texttt{mDeBERTa-v3}, and 91.46 for \texttt{XLM-R}; each retained encoder also exceeds \texttt{BERTi\'{c}} on every seed.
Given this consistent validation gap and the computational cost of running another complete configuration grid, we do not carry \texttt{BERTi\'{c}} into the main experiments.
%For those experiments, the sweep selects a learning rate of $2 \times 10^{-5}$ for all three retained encoders, with dropout 0.05 for \texttt{mmBERT} and 0.10 for \texttt{mDeBERTa-v3} and \texttt{XLM-R}; the selected setting for each encoder is then fixed across all data configurations.
%Here, dropout denotes classifier-head dropout for \texttt{mmBERT} and \texttt{XLM-R}; for \texttt{mDeBERTa-v3}, the Transformers implementation maps this setting to hidden dropout, which also affects the encoder layers.
For these experiments, the sweep selects a learning rate of $2 \times 10^{-5}$ for all three retained encoders, with dropout set to 0.05 for \texttt{mmBERT} and 0.10 for \texttt{mDeBERTa-v3} and \texttt{XLM-R}.
The selected setting for each encoder is then fixed across all data configurations.
Here, dropout refers to classifier-head dropout for \texttt{mmBERT} and \texttt{XLM-R}. For \texttt{mDeBERTa-v3}, the Transformers implementation maps this setting to hidden dropout, which also affects the encoder layers.

For the final experiments, we use AdamW~\parencite{loshchilov2019decoupledweightdecayregularization} with linear learning-rate decay, a warmup ratio of 0.06, weight decay of 0.01, and a maximum gradient norm of 1.0.
All models use a maximum sequence length of 256 tokens, a per-device training batch size of 16, and two gradient-accumulation steps, giving an effective batch size of 32.
The per-device evaluation batch size is 16 for \texttt{mmBERT} and \texttt{XLM-R} and 32 for \texttt{mDeBERTa-v3}.
Models are trained in FP32 for 20 epochs, evaluated and checkpointed after each epoch, and restored to the checkpoint with the best configuration-specific validation F1-score.
We evaluate 50 data configurations for each of the three retained encoders.
Repeating every encoder--configuration pair with three random seeds yields 450 final models.
For each repeat, the same seed value is passed separately to data splitting, multilingual sentence sampling, and model training, producing matched seed-specific datasets across encoder comparisons rather than a shared random-number stream.
Eight data configurations per encoder use the two-stage \texttt{Pretrain-Multi7-full-L} procedure, in which the intermediate multilingual models constitute additional training stages and are not included in the count of 450 final models.

Checkpoint selection follows each configuration's evaluation scope.
For monolingual models, reduced-resource profile runs, \texttt{Multi8-full-L}, and the final adaptation stage of \texttt{Pretrain-Multi7-full-L}, we select the checkpoint with the highest target-language validation F1-score.
The 100\% multilingual profile endpoints reuse the standard \texttt{Multi-8} and \texttt{Multi-12} checkpoints rather than selecting separate target-specific checkpoints.
For \texttt{Multi-8}, \texttt{Multi-12}, and the full-pool shared models, model selection is based on validation F1 macro-averaged across $L_8$.
Auxiliary languages contribute only to training and are excluded from the selection criterion.
The Croatian source-quality models use macro-averaged validation F1 over $L_7$.
In the first stage of \texttt{Pretrain-Multi7-full-L}, checkpoint selection uses macro-averaged validation F1 over the seven non-target source languages before target-language adaptation.

\subsection{Evaluation}
We use \texttt{seqeval}~\parencite{seqeval-2018} entity-level micro F1, which requires an exact match of both entity span and type.
For every configuration and language, we compute the mean and population standard deviation over three random-seed runs.
This variation combines changes in the data split, multilingual sampling, and model training.
To keep the main tables compact, their score panels report mean macro F1 without standard deviations, alongside the RQ-aligned contrasts.
Complete language-level means and standard deviations, together with the corresponding macro standard deviations, are provided in the supplementary material.
For the main comparisons, target-specific recovery experiments, and full-pool models, macro F1 is the unweighted arithmetic mean over $L_8$.
The Croatian source-quality ablation analogously averages over $L_7$, while the target-resource profiles report only the selected target language.
Thus, ``macro F1'' denotes an average of language-level entity-micro F1 scores rather than an entity-type macro score. All core evaluations use the three entity types \texttt{LOC}, \texttt{ORG}, and \texttt{PER}.

For each retained encoder separately, we evaluate ten RQ-aligned configuration contrasts using paired language-level F1 means, with each language score first averaged over the three seeds.
The Croatian source-quality contrast has seven paired observations, while all other tested contrasts have eight.
Alongside the mean F1-point difference and direction count, we use the exact two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test as the primary language-level test because it accounts for ranked difference magnitudes.
We additionally report an exact two-sided sign test as a magnitude-free robustness check based only on the direction of the differences. Both tests omit zero differences.
The reported $p$-values are exploratory and use an unadjusted $\alpha=0.05$ without correction for multiple comparisons.
We do not apply these tests to the target-resource profiles, nor do we make per-language significance claims based on only three runs.
Instead, those results are interpreted through effect size, direction, and run-to-run variation.

\section{Discussion: practical guidance}
\label{sec:discussion}

The results suggest four practical decisions.
First, target-rich training should preserve all target-language evidence. Full-target models recover the balanced deficit without reliably outperforming monolingual specialisation.
Second, clean balanced transfer is most valuable under genuine scarcity. Serbian benefits strongly at low budgets, but its full-data result depends on the encoder.
Third, manually curated and automatically constructed corpora are not interchangeable. Manual Croatian supervision transfers consistently better under the nominally matched budget, while the broader \texttt{Multi-12} result also reflects a lower main-language quota.
Fourth, \texttt{Full-Multi8} is the strongest shared deployment recipe tested. It is practically competitive with separate monolingual models, whereas adding or capping the lower-confidence auxiliary sources provides no consistent benefit.

Leave-one-target-out pretraining is an encoder-dependent alternative. Its \texttt{mDeBERTa-v3} advantage has Wilcoxon but not sign-test support and does not replicate for the other encoders. Joint full-target training is therefore the more consistent recovery strategy.
Overall, multilingual NER depends not on language count alone, but on target evidence, source compatibility, encoder, and sampling strategy.

Differences in datasets, supervision settings, label spaces, and evaluation protocols limit direct numerical comparison with earlier Slavic NER studies.
\textcite{ulcar2026monocross} report XLM-R three-class macro F1 scores of 83.3 on HR500K and 91.2 on SSJ500K under target-language fine-tuning, together with zero-shot scores of 76.9 for Slovene-to-Croatian transfer and 88.2 for Croatian-to-Slovene transfer.
Our corresponding XLM-R monolingual means are 92.64 for Croatian and 93.20 for Slovene, while the eight-language macro average of the shared \texttt{Full-Multi8} model is 93.56.
However, our evaluation uses exact-span micro F1 over position-anchored entity mentions, deduplicated random sentence-level splits, and a pooled multi-corpus Slovene target.
Moreover, our shared models retain target-language supervision and therefore do not instantiate the zero-shot setting evaluated by \textcite{ulcar2026monocross}.

The 2021 and 2023 Slav-NER shared tasks report leading recognition performance around 90 F1~\parencite{piskorski-etal-2021-slav,yangarber-etal-2023-slav}, but evaluate the extraction of unique entity forms per document rather than position-anchored exact spans.
On the subsequently curated cross-lingual Slavic corpus, \textcite{piskorski-etal-2024-cross} report 92.22 micro F1 for an in-domain cross-topic XLM-R-large baseline over six languages and five entity types, compared with 85.03 when one topic is held out.
These results provide contextual calibration only: differences in model size, label space, corpus composition, splitting procedure, evaluation unit, and averaging prevent direct ranking of the systems.

The study is limited to three base-sized multilingual encoders and aggregate entity-level F1.
Type-specific PER, LOC, and ORG metrics are reported in the supplementary material. Still, we do not analyse how transfer effects vary systematically by entity type, entity length, frequency, or ambiguity.
Random sentence-level splits do not test document, temporal, topical, or cross-corpus shifts.
The Slovene profiles are not target-token matched and cannot isolate a causal transfer effect as target data increase.
The Croatian ablation covers one language and two sources under nominal matching, and those sources also differ in domain, segmentation, density, and annotation process.
Likewise, \texttt{Multi-8} versus \texttt{Multi-12} combines language addition, provenance, and quota reduction.
Finally, the tests use only seven or eight language observations and an unadjusted exploratory threshold, so broader confirmation is needed.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Across three encoders, balanced \texttt{Multi-8} trails monolingual training by 1.56--1.85 macro F1 points overall, yet gains 4.32--7.53 points for Serbian at the 10\% target budget.
The benefit narrows and becomes encoder-dependent at full target data.
The balanced \texttt{Multi-12} recipe is worse on every evaluation language and encoder, although this comparison combines auxiliary-source inclusion with a substantially lower main-language quota.
In the separate nominally token-matched Croatian source ablation, HR500K transfers more effectively than WikiANN/PAN-X by 0.24--0.45 macro F1 points across encoders.

Retaining all target data recovers the balanced deficit and reaches monolingual-level performance without a reliable overall advantage.
Leave-one-target-out pretraining provides a small Wilcoxon-supported benefit only for \texttt{mDeBERTa-v3} and is neutral for \texttt{mmBERT} and \texttt{XLM-R}.
For one shared model, \texttt{Full-Multi8} reaches 93.62 macro F1 for \texttt{mmBERT}, 94.02 for \texttt{mDeBERTa-v3}, and 93.56 for \texttt{XLM-R}, making it descriptively the strongest full-pool configuration for every encoder.
Adding or capping lower-confidence auxiliary data gives no consistent improvement.

Multilingual transfer is therefore most useful under genuine target-data scarcity.
With sufficient target data, preserve it rather than enforce balance.
Treat source compatibility explicitly, and validate lower-confidence auxiliary corpora before including them in a shared full-pool model.

Two \texttt{mDeBERTa-v3-base} NER checkpoints intended for broader use are available at \url{https://huggingface.co/ivlcic/snerta-8l-base} and \url{https://huggingface.co/ivlcic/snerta-12l-base}.
The former was trained on the eight manually annotated language datasets, while the latter additionally uses four WikiANN/PAN-X auxiliary-language datasets.
Unlike the controlled experiments reported in this paper, both checkpoints retain \texttt{MISC} as a fourth entity type rather than mapping it to \texttt{O}.

\titleeng{Kdaj je večjezični prenos koristen za prepoznavanje imenskih entitet v slovanskih jezikih?}
\begin{abstracteng}
V prispevku preučujemo, kdaj je večjezični prenos v praksi koristen za prepoznavanje imenskih entitet v slovanskih jezikih (NER).
Heterogenim korpusom dvanajstih jezikov uskladimo označevalno shemo IOB2 s tremi skupnimi tipi entitet: LOC, ORG in PER.
Poskuse smo izvedli s pomočjo treh Transformer kodirnikov na ročno označenih podatkovnih zbirkah osmih jezikov, ter na pomožnih samodejno zgrajenih korpusih z nižjo stopnjo zaupanja za dodatne štiri jezike.
Analiziramo vplive večjezičnega združevanja, kakovosti podatkov (ročno označene v primerjavi s samodejno zgrajenimi podatkovnimi zbirkami), obsega virov za ciljni jezik in ohranjanja učinkov ciljnega jezika.
Z izklapljanjem znotraj hrvaškega jezika, pri enakem nominalnem številu pojavnic primerjamo ročno označeno zbirko HR500K s samodejno zgrajeno zbirko WikiANN/PAN-X.
Preverjamo, ali lahko uravnotežen skupni večjezični model nadomesti močne enojezične izhodiščne modele, ali pomožni korpusi z nižjo stopnjo zaupanja pomagajo ali škodujejo, kako se prenos spreminja ob zmanjšani količini učnih podatkov za ciljni jezik in ali lahko večjezično učenje z vsemi podatki ciljnega jezika, oziroma z vsemi podatki v združenem naboru, zmanjša razliko v primerjavi z enojezičnimi modeli.
Rezultati kažejo, da uravnoteženo večjezično učenje ni univerzalna zamenjava za enojezično specializacijo, lahko pa izrazito koristi ciljnim jezikom z manj viri.
Pri uporabi uravnoteženja podatkov vključevanje pomožnih korpusov z nižjo stopnjo zaupanja kot enakovredno uteženih učnih podatkov dosledno poslabša uspešnost, medtem ko ohranitev vseh podatkov ciljnega jezika večjezičnim modelom ponovno vrne konkurenčnost.
V tej postavitvi je večjezični prenos najzanesljivejši, kadar ohranimo vse podatke ciljnega jezika in heterogene pomožne vire vključujemo selektivno.
\end{abstracteng}
